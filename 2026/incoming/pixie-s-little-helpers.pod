=head2 The sticky note

Pixie the Elf (whose parents had strange ideas about names) had a sticky note
on the edge of her monitor. It had been there so long that the glue had given
up and she'd had to reattach it with sticky tape. Twice.

The note was headed "ONE DAY" and it listed all the little jobs she was
going to automate when she finally had a spare afternoon:

=for :list
* Diff the Naughty and Nice exports
* Fix the photo filenames (!!!)
* Check the newsletter links before it goes out
* Make the expenses stop being a nightmare

None of them was a big job. Each one cost her maybe ten or fifteen minutes of
tedium a week. But each one would also take half a day to write properly.
And in the run-up to Christmas, nobody at the North Pole has half a day to.
spare. So the note stayed on the monitor and the jobs stayed tedious.

This is the story of how that sticky note finally ended up in the recycling.

=head2 Meet Tinsel

In October, the Workshop's Platform team (led by an extremely enthusiastic
elf called Jingle) rolled out a new tool to everyone in Engineering. It was a
coding agent called Tinsel.

"It's not like those chatbots," Jingle explained at the all-hands. "It lives
in your terminal. You start it up in a project directory and tell it what you
want in plain English. It reads your code, runs your tests, and suggests
changes. It asks before it runs anything or touches any files. You stay in
charge."

Pixie was sceptical. She'd spent most of last December cleaning up after a
junior elf who had pasted chatbot code straight into the sleigh routing
system - code which cheerfully used a CPAN module called
C<Sleigh::Route::Optimal>. That module did not exist. It had never existed.
The chatbot had hallucinated it because it sounded plausible.

But Jingle was persuasive (and was handing out candy canes), so Pixie decided
to give Tinsel a fair trial. She started with something safe.

=head3 Test one: explain this

Deep in the Workshop's repository there was a program called C<manifest.pl>.
It built the loading manifest for the sleigh every Christmas Eve. It had been
written in 2003 by an elf who had since retired to a cabin in Lapland, and
nobody else really understood it. Every year, someone changed one line with
their eyes closed and hoped for the best.

Pixie opened a terminal in the repository, started Tinsel and typed:

  Read manifest.pl and explain to me what it does, section by
  section. Don't change anything.

A minute later she had a clear, accurate, section-by-section explanation of
four hundred lines of Perl that she'd been avoiding for three years. Better
still, Tinsel had noticed something:

  One thing worth flagging: on line 212, the year is calculated as
  (localtime)[5] + 1900, but on line 287 the code uses (localtime)[5]
  directly when building the archive filename. That's why the
  archived manifests have names like manifest-126.txt.

Pixie checked the archive directory. There, in all their glory, were
C<manifest-123.txt>, C<manifest-124.txt> and C<manifest-125.txt>. She'd
always assumed someone had done that on purpose.

=head3 Test two: fix this

Next, she pointed Tinsel at a test in the NaughtyOrNice application that had
been failing on and off for a fortnight.

  t/scoring.t fails sometimes but not always. Find out why and
  suggest a fix. Show me the diff before you change anything.

Tinsel asked permission to run C<prove -lv t/scoring.t>. It ran it several
times, noticed that it only failed when two children had exactly the same
niceness score, and tracked it down to a C<sort> that didn't specify a
tie-breaker - so the order depended on Perl's hash ordering, which is
deliberately randomised. It proposed a two-line patch that sorted by name
when the scores were equal, and added a test for this case.

Pixie read the diff carefully. It was exactly the fix she'd have written -
if she'd ever found the time to look.

=head3 House rules

By now Pixie was cautiously impressed. But she'd been programming long enough
to know that a tool that's right most of the time is a tool you need to keep
an eye on. Tinsel could read a file in the project directory called
C<TINSEL.md> at the start of every session, so she wrote down some house
rules:

  # How we write Perl here

  - Target Perl 5.36. Start every program with `use v5.36;`
    (which gives us strict, warnings and signatures).
  - Use well-known CPAN modules rather than writing your own
    parsers. CSV means Text::CSV_XS. Never split CSV on commas.
  - Only use modules that actually exist. If you're not sure,
    check https://metacpan.org/ before using it.
  - Anything that changes or deletes files must have a dry-run
    mode, and dry-run is the default.
  - Keep scripts short and readable. Comment the "why", not the
    "what".
  - Always show me the diff before changing existing code.

It turned out that the rules made a big difference. Tinsel was perfectly
capable of writing good Perl; it just needed to be told what "good" meant
I<here>. Exactly like a new elf joining the team, really.

And that was when Pixie looked at her sticky note.

=head2 Helper one: the Naughty-and-Nice List Differ

Every night, the NaughtyOrNice system dumps the whole list to a CSV file. And
every morning, someone from the Department of Behavioural Assessment emails
Pixie to ask "who changed yesterday?" Pixie had been answering by opening two
files side by side and squinting.

She typed:

  Write a script called nice-diff that takes two of the daily
  CSV exports (columns: child_id, name, status) and tells me who
  was added, who was removed and whose status changed.

Here's what she ended up with:

  use v5.36;
  use Text::CSV_XS qw(csv);

  # Usage: nice-diff yesterday.csv today.csv
  @ARGV == 2 or die "Usage: $0 OLD.csv NEW.csv\n";
  my ($old_file, $new_file) = @ARGV;

  # Each export has a header row: child_id,name,status
  my $old = csv(in => $old_file, key => 'child_id') or die Text::CSV_XS->error_diag;
  my $new = csv(in => $new_file, key => 'child_id') or die Text::CSV_XS->error_diag;

  my (@added, @removed, @changed);

  for my $id (sort keys %$new) {
    if (!exists $old->{$id}) {
      push @added, $new->{$id};
    } elsif ($old->{$id}{status} ne $new->{$id}{status}) {
      push @changed, [ $old->{$id}, $new->{$id} ];
    }
  }

  for my $id (sort keys %$old) {
    push @removed, $old->{$id} unless exists $new->{$id};
  }

  say 'Added (', scalar @added, '):';
  say "  + $_->{name} ($_->{status})" for @added;

  say 'Removed (', scalar @removed, '):';
  say "  - $_->{name} (was $_->{status})" for @removed;

  say 'Changed status (', scalar @changed, '):';
  say "  * $_->[1]{name}: $_->[0]{status} -> $_->[1]{status}" for @changed;


The C<csv()> function from L<Text::CSV_XS|https://metacpan.org/pod/Text::CSV_XS>
does almost all of the work. Give it a C<key> and it returns a hash of
hashes, keyed on that column. After that, it's just a couple of loops.

  $ nice-diff 2026-12-01.csv 2026-12-02.csv
  Added (1):
    + Noel Nutmeg (Nice)
  Removed (1):
    - Billy Bauble (was Naughty)
  Changed status (1):
    * Timmy Tinsel: Nice -> Naughty

(No relation to the coding agent, apparently. Pixie checked.)

It's worth knowing that this I<wasn't> Tinsel's first draft. Before Pixie
wrote her house rules, Tinsel's version read the files with C<split /,/>.
That works right up to the moment you meet a child called "Holly, Jr." — and
the North Pole has I<lots> of children with commas in their names. Once the
rule was in C<TINSEL.md>, Tinsel never made that mistake again.

Writing it, reviewing it and testing it took about ten minutes. Pixie
crossed the first line off her sticky note.

=head2 Helper two: the Terrible Filename Fixer

The Workshop photographers are lovely elves who take beautiful pictures. They
are also completely incapable of naming a file sensibly. Pixie's shared photo
folder was full of things like C<IMG_7832.jpg>, C<DSC00421.JPG> and the dreaded
C<final-final-2.jpg>.

The information to fix this I<did> exist. The cameras record when every
photo was taken, and the photographers (grudgingly) fill in a spreadsheet
saying which event each photo was from and who's in it. It's just that
nobody ever had time to put the two together.

  Write a script called fix-photo-names. For every image in a
  directory, get the date it was taken from the EXIF data, and
  the event and subject from photos.csv (columns: filename,
  event, subject). Rename it to something like
  2026-12-03_toy-testing_rudolph.jpg. Never overwrite a file.

Tinsel produced this:

  use v5.36;
  use File::Basename qw(fileparse);
  use File::Spec::Functions qw(catfile);
  use Getopt::Long qw(GetOptions);
  use Image::ExifTool;
  use POSIX qw(strftime);
  use Text::CSV_XS qw(csv);

  # Usage: fix-photo-names [--go] [--csv photos.csv] DIR
  # Without --go, it just tells you what it *would* do.
  GetOptions(
    'go'    => \my $go,
    'csv=s' => \(my $csv_file = 'photos.csv'),
  ) or die "Usage: $0 [--go] [--csv FILE] DIR\n";

  my $dir = shift // '.';

  # photos.csv: filename,event,subject
  my $info = csv(in => $csv_file, key => 'filename') or die Text::CSV_XS->error_diag;

  my $exif = Image::ExifTool->new;
  my %taken;  # new names we've already used

  opendir my $dh, $dir or die "Can't open $dir: $!\n";

  for my $file (sort grep { /\.(jpe?g|png|heic)\z/i } readdir $dh) {
    # Already fixed on a previous run? Leave it alone.
    next if $file =~ /^\d{4}-\d\d-\d\d_/;

    my $path = catfile($dir, $file);
    my (undef, undef, $ext) = fileparse($file, qr/\.[^.]+\z/);
    $ext = lc $ext;
    $ext = '.jpg' if $ext eq '.jpeg';

    # When was it taken? Ask the EXIF data, fall back to the file's mtime.
    $exif->ExtractInfo($path);
    my $when = $exif->GetValue('DateTimeOriginal')
            // $exif->GetValue('CreateDate');
    my $date = $when && $when =~ /^(\d{4}):(\d\d):(\d\d)/
             ? "$1-$2-$3"
             : strftime('%Y-%m-%d', localtime((stat $path)[9]));

    # What is it? Ask the CSV.
    my $row  = $info->{$file};
    my @bits = $row ? @$row{qw(event subject)} : ('unsorted');
    my $stem = join '_', $date, map { slugify($_) } grep { length } @bits;

    # Never overwrite anything - add a counter if we need to.
    my $new = "$stem$ext";
    my $n   = 1;
    $new = "${stem}_" . ++$n . $ext
      while $taken{$new} || ($new ne $file && -e catfile($dir, $new));
    $taken{$new} = 1;

    next if $new eq $file;

    say "$file -> $new";
    if ($go) {
      rename $path, catfile($dir, $new) or warn "  Couldn't rename $file: $!\n";
    }
  }

  say "\n(Dry run: use --go to actually rename things)" unless $go;

  sub slugify ($str) {
    $str = lc $str;
    $str =~ s/[^a-z0-9]+/-/g;
    $str =~ s/^-|-$//g;
    return $str;
  }


This one leans on L<Image::ExifTool|https://metacpan.org/pod/Image::ExifTool>,
which can read metadata from just about any image format ever invented. It
also follows the house rules: by default it only tells you what it I<would>
do.

  $ fix-photo-names photos
  DSC00421.JPG -> 2026-12-03_sleigh-polish_dasher-dancer.jpg
  IMG_7832.jpg -> 2026-12-03_toy-testing_rudolph.jpg
  IMG_7833.jpeg -> 2026-12-03_toy-testing_rudolph_2.jpg
  final-final-2.jpg -> 2026-11-28_staff-party_mrs-claus.jpg
  mystery.png -> 2026-11-30_unsorted.png

  (Dry run: use --go to actually rename things)

That dry run earned its keep. The first version didn't have the "Already
fixed?" check near the top of the loop. Pixie ran it with C<--go>, then ran
it again (as you do) to make sure it was happy and the dry run cheerfully
announced that it was going to rename every single photo to
C<2026-12-03_unsorted.jpg>, C<2026-12-03_unsorted_2.jpg> and so on. Of
course: the newly renamed files weren't in the CSV any more.

She explained the problem to Tinsel, which apologised (it was very polite
like that) and added the two-line fix. The important thing is that the bug
was caught by a I<dry run>, not by a photographer.

Line two: crossed off.

=head2 Helper three: the Newsletter Link Checker

Every Friday, the Workshop sends out a newsletter: I<Workshop Weekly>. And
every Friday afternoon, someone replies to tell Pixie that one of the links
is broken. The reindeer rota was a particular favourite - it moved to a new
location roughly once a month.

  Write a script called check-links that finds every link and
  image in an HTML newsletter, checks each one and reports which
  ones are broken and which ones redirect somewhere else. Exit
  with a non-zero status if anything is broken.

That last requirement was Pixie being clever. A script that exits with an
error when something is wrong can go straight into the newsletter's
L<GitHub Actions|https://perladvent.org/2024/2024-12-04.html> workflow, so
nobody can send out a newsletter with broken links ever again.

  use v5.36;
  use HTML::LinkExtor;
  use HTTP::Tiny;
  use URI;

  # Usage: check-links newsletter.html [base_url]
  my $file = shift or die "Usage: $0 FILE.html [BASE_URL]\n";
  my $base = shift // 'https://northpole.example/newsletter/';

  # Collect every link and image in the newsletter, made absolute.
  my %urls;
  my $extor = HTML::LinkExtor->new(sub ($tag, %attr) {
    for my $url (values %attr) {
      my $abs = URI->new_abs($url, $base);
      $urls{ $abs->canonical } = 1 if $abs->scheme =~ /^https?\z/;
    }
  });
  $extor->parse_file($file) or die "Can't parse $file: $!\n";

  my $http = HTTP::Tiny->new(
    agent        => 'PixieLinkChecker/1.0 ',
    timeout      => 10,
    max_redirect => 5,
  );

  my (@ok, @moved, @broken);

  for my $url (sort keys %urls) {
    # HEAD is cheaper, but some servers don't like it - so retry with GET.
    my $res = $http->head($url);
    $res = $http->get($url) if $res->{status} == 405 || $res->{status} == 501;

    if (!$res->{success}) {
      my $why = $res->{status} == 599 ? $res->{content} : "$res->{status} $res->{reason}";
      chomp $why;
      push @broken, "$url\n      $why";
    } elsif ($res->{redirects}) {
      push @moved, "$url\n      -> $res->{url}";
    } else {
      push @ok, $url;
    }
  }

  printf "Checked %d links: %d OK, %d redirected, %d broken\n\n",
    scalar keys %urls, scalar @ok, scalar @moved, scalar @broken;

  say "BROKEN:\n", map { "  $_\n" } @broken if @broken;
  say "REDIRECTED (consider updating):\n", map { "  $_\n" } @moved if @moved;

  exit(@broken ? 1 : 0);


L<HTML::LinkExtor|https://metacpan.org/pod/HTML::LinkExtor> finds every URL
in the document (from C<< <a href> >>, C<< <img src> >> and friends),
L<URI|https://metacpan.org/pod/URI> turns relative links into absolute ones,
and L<HTTP::Tiny|https://metacpan.org/pod/HTTP::Tiny> — which has been in
the Perl core since 5.14 — does the checking. HTTP::Tiny follows redirects
for you and keeps a list of them in C<< $res->{redirects} >>, so spotting
links that have moved is trivial. (If your links use C<https> — and they
should — you'll need L<IO::Socket::SSL|https://metacpan.org/pod/IO::Socket::SSL>
installed too.)

Pixie's one contribution to the code was the C<HEAD>-then-C<GET> dance. The
Elf HR system refuses C<HEAD> requests with a C<405 Method Not Allowed>,
because of course it does. Tinsel's original version reported every HR link
as broken.

  $ check-links newsletter.html
  Checked 5 links: 2 OK, 1 redirected, 2 broken

  BROKEN:
    https://northpole.example/gone/rota.pdf
        404 Not Found
    https://wiki.northpole.example/nobody-home
        Could not connect to 'wiki.northpole.example:443': Connection refused

  REDIRECTED (consider updating):
    https://northpole.example/old-sleigh
        -> https://northpole.example/sleigh-tracker

The reindeer rota had moved again.

=head2 Helper four: the Reindeer Expenses Wrangler

And so to the last line of the sticky note - the one with three underlines.

Every month, Pixie collected the expenses from three teams and passed them on
to Accounts. And every month, each team sent them in a different format:

=over 4

=item *

The Reindeer Barn used an Excel spreadsheet, with British-style dates and
everything in Norwegian kroner.

=item *

The Toy Workshop sent a CSV file with ISO dates, a currency column (which
they sometimes forgot to fill in) and thousands separators in the amounts.

=item *

The Wrapping Department sent a TSV file. Nobody knew why. Their dates
looked like "3 Dec 2026" and their amounts looked like "€1.234,50".

=back

Accounts, naturally, wanted one CSV file in a completely different format.
Pixie usually spent an hour every month with a spreadsheet, a calculator and
a large mug of cocoa.

  Write a script called wrangle-expenses that takes any number of
  expense files from these three teams (I've put examples in
  data/), works out which team each file came from by looking at
  its header row, and writes a single CSV for Accounts with the
  columns date, team, description, amount, currency. Dates should
  be ISO 8601 and amounts should be plain numbers.

Tinsel gave her this:

  use v5.36;
  use utf8;                             # there's a € in this file...
  use open qw(:std :encoding(UTF-8));   # ...and in the output
  use Spreadsheet::Read qw(ReadData rows);
  use Text::CSV_XS qw(csv);
  use Time::Piece;

  # Usage: wrangle-expenses FILE... > for-accounts.csv
  #
  # Every team sends their expenses in a different shape. Each entry here
  # describes one shape: how to recognise it (the header row), which column
  # holds what, and how to read its dates and money.
  my @formats = (
    {
      team     => 'Reindeer Barn',
      headers  => [ 'Date', 'Item', 'Cost (NOK)' ],
      columns  => { date => 'Date', description => 'Item', amount => 'Cost (NOK)' },
      date_fmt => '%d/%m/%Y',
      currency => 'NOK',
    },
    {
      team     => 'Toy Workshop',
      headers  => [ 'when', 'what', 'amount', 'currency' ],
      columns  => { date => 'when', description => 'what',
                    amount => 'amount', currency => 'currency' },
      date_fmt => '%Y-%m-%d',
      currency => 'USD',    # when they forget to fill that column in
    },
    {
      team          => 'Wrapping Department',
      headers       => [ 'Expense Date', 'Description', 'Total' ],
      columns       => { date => 'Expense Date', description => 'Description',
                         amount => 'Total' },
      date_fmt      => '%d %b %Y',
      decimal_comma => 1,   # they write 12,50 - not 12.50
    },
  );

  my %symbol = ('€' => 'EUR', '£' => 'GBP', '$' => 'USD', 'kr' => 'NOK');

  my @out;

  for my $file (@ARGV) {
    my %opt  = $file =~ /\.tsv\z/i ? (parser => 'csv', sep => "\t") : ();
    my $book = ReadData($file, strip => 3, %opt)
      or die "Can't read $file\n";
    my ($header, @rows) = rows($book->[1]);

    my $fmt = identify($header)
      or die "$file: I don't recognise the columns [@$header]\n";
    warn "$file: looks like $fmt->{team}\n";

    my %col;   # header name => column index
    @col{@$header} = 0 .. $#$header;

    for my $row (@rows) {
      next unless grep { defined && length } @$row;   # skip blank lines
      my %rec = map { $_ => $row->[ $col{ $fmt->{columns}{$_} } ] }
                keys %{ $fmt->{columns} };

      my ($amount, $currency) = money($rec{amount}, $fmt);
      push @out, {
        date        => Time::Piece->strptime($rec{date}, $fmt->{date_fmt})->ymd,
        team        => $fmt->{team},
        description => $rec{description},
        amount      => $amount,
        currency    => uc($rec{currency} || $currency || $fmt->{currency} || '???'),
      };
    }
  }

  # This is the one format Accounts actually wants.
  csv(
    in      => [ sort { $a->{date} cmp $b->{date} } @out ],
    out     => \*STDOUT,
    headers => [ qw(date team description amount currency) ],
  );

  sub identify ($header) {
    my $got = join "\0", map { lc } @$header;
    for my $fmt (@formats) {
      return $fmt if $got eq join "\0", map { lc } @{ $fmt->{headers} };
    }
    return;
  }

  sub money ($str, $fmt) {
    my $currency;
    for my $sym (keys %symbol) {
      $currency = $symbol{$sym} if $str =~ s/\Q$sym\E//;
    }
    $str =~ s/\s+//g;
    if ($fmt->{decimal_comma}) {
      $str =~ tr/.//d;     # 1.234,50 -> 1234,50
      $str =~ tr/,/./;     # 1234,50  -> 1234.50
    } else {
      $str =~ tr/,//d;     # 1,234.50 -> 1234.50
    }
    return (sprintf('%.2f', $str), $currency);
  }


The clever bit here isn't Pixie's I<or> Tinsel's - it's
L<Spreadsheet::Read|https://metacpan.org/pod/Spreadsheet::Read>. It gives you
the same interface whether you're reading an Excel file, a LibreOffice file,
a CSV or (with a little persuasion) a TSV. Behind the scenes, it picks the
right parser for the job (L<Spreadsheet::ParseXLSX|https://metacpan.org/pod/Spreadsheet::ParseXLSX>
for the reindeer, Text::CSV_XS for everyone else).

The design is nice, too. Each team's format is just a hash in the
C<@formats> array, so when the Elf HR department inevitably starts sending in
expenses as well, Pixie will only need to add another entry.

  $ wrangle-expenses reindeer-barn.xlsx toy-workshop.csv wrapping.tsv
  reindeer-barn.xlsx: looks like Reindeer Barn
  toy-workshop.csv: looks like Toy Workshop
  wrapping.tsv: looks like Wrapping Department
  date,team,description,amount,currency
  2026-12-01,"Reindeer Barn","Premium lichen",450.00,NOK
  2026-12-01,"Toy Workshop","Spare springs",89.99,USD
  2026-12-01,"Wrapping Department","Sticky tape",12.50,EUR
  2026-12-02,"Reindeer Barn","Harness polish, extra shiny",1200.00,NOK
  2026-12-02,"Toy Workshop","Glitter (industrial)",2350.75,USD
  2026-12-03,"Wrapping Department","Ribbon (red)",1234.50,EUR
  2026-12-04,"Reindeer Barn","Antler wax",1050.00,NOK

Once again, it wasn't perfect first time. The first run said that the
Wrapping Department had spent precisely 0.00 on everything, along with a
couple of C<isn't numeric> warnings. The script had a C<€> in its source
code, but no C<use utf8>, so Perl was looking for the three bytes of a UTF-8
euro sign while Spreadsheet::Read was handing it a single, properly decoded
character. Pixie spotted it because she I<always> reads the output before
she sends it to Accounts. Tinsel added the C<use utf8> and C<use open> lines
and, at Pixie's request, replaced a silent default currency with a very
unsilent C<???>, so Accounts would notice if anything slipped through.

Line four: crossed off. With three underlines.

=head2 What Pixie learned

On the afternoon of the 23rd, Santa wandered past Pixie's desk with a mince
pie and noticed that something was missing.

"Wasn't there a sticky note there?" he asked. "You've had it for years."

"It's gone," said Pixie. "I finally did all the jobs on it."

"All of them? When did you find the time?"

Pixie thought about that for a moment.

"I didn't," she said. "That's the point. None of those jobs was ever hard.
They just weren't I<worth> it. Half a day to save fifteen minutes a week,
right before Christmas? Never going to happen. But ten minutes to save fifteen
minutes a week? That's a no-brainer."

"So the elf-in-the-terminal wrote them all for you?"

"Tinsel wrote the first drafts," said Pixie. "I decided what they should do,
I set the rules, I read every line, and I caught the bugs. If I didn't know
Perl, I wouldn't have spotted the comma in Holly Jr.'s name, or the
renaming disaster, or the euro sign. And notice how short they all are?
That's not because Tinsel is clever; it's because someone already wrote
Text::CSV_XS, and Image::ExifTool, and HTTP::Tiny, and Spreadsheet::Read.
Tinsel just knew where to find them."

She took a bite of Santa's mince pie while he wasn't looking.

"Here's the thing," she said. "LLMs have lowered the minimum size of a
problem that's worth solving with code."

Santa nodded slowly, in the way he did when he was going to pass something
off as his own idea at the next all-hands.

"Very good," he said. "In that case, you won't mind looking at the sleigh
routing code. I've got a feeling someone's been using a module that doesn't
exist."

Pixie sighed, reached for a pad of sticky notes, and started a new list.

=head2 Your own little helpers

You probably have a sticky note of your own - a list of small, annoying jobs
that have never quite been worth automating. If you've got access to a coding
agent, it might be time to revisit that list. A few tips from Pixie:

=for :list
* Write down your house rules. Your agent doesn't know your conventions until you tell it, and a short file of rules saves a lot of correcting.
* Insist on CPAN. A coding agent that reaches for Text::CSV_XS rather than C<split> is writing code you'll be happy to maintain.
